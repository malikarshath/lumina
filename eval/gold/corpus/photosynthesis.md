# Photosynthesis: Light-Dependent and Light-Independent Reactions

Photosynthesis is the process by which plants, algae, and some bacteria convert light energy into
chemical energy stored in glucose. In plants, photosynthesis takes place primarily in the chloroplast,
an organelle containing a stack of membrane sacs called thylakoids, surrounded by a fluid-filled space
called the stroma. The process is divided into two linked stages: the light-dependent reactions, which
occur in the thylakoid membrane, and the light-independent reactions, also called the Calvin cycle,
which occur in the stroma.

## Light-dependent reactions

The light-dependent reactions begin when chlorophyll and other pigments embedded in the thylakoid
membrane absorb light energy. This energy is captured by two protein complexes called photosystem II
and photosystem I. In photosystem II, absorbed light energy splits water molecules in a process called
photolysis, releasing electrons, protons, and oxygen gas as a byproduct. This is the source of nearly
all of the oxygen in Earth's atmosphere. The freed electrons travel through an electron transport chain
embedded in the thylakoid membrane, losing energy as they move, and this energy is used to pump protons
into the thylakoid space, creating a proton gradient.

The electrons eventually reach photosystem I, where they are re-energized by more absorbed light and
passed to the electron carrier NADP+, reducing it to NADPH. Meanwhile, the proton gradient built up
across the thylakoid membrane drives protons back through an enzyme called ATP synthase, in a process
called photophosphorylation, generating ATP. The two products of the light-dependent reactions, ATP
and NADPH, are then used to power the light-independent reactions.

## The Calvin cycle

The Calvin cycle, named after Melvin Calvin who discovered it, takes place in the stroma and does not
directly require light, though it depends on the ATP and NADPH produced by the light-dependent
reactions. The cycle begins with carbon fixation: the enzyme RuBisCO (ribulose-1,5-bisphosphate
carboxylase/oxygenase) attaches a molecule of carbon dioxide from the atmosphere to a five-carbon
sugar called RuBP (ribulose bisphosphate), forming an unstable six-carbon intermediate that immediately
splits into two molecules of 3-phosphoglycerate (3-PGA).

In the reduction phase, ATP and NADPH from the light-dependent reactions convert 3-PGA into
glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate (G3P). Most of the G3P produced is used to regenerate RuBP so the cycle can
continue, in a process that also consumes ATP. For every three turns of the cycle, one net molecule of
G3P is released for use in building glucose and other carbohydrates; six turns of the cycle, fixing six
carbon dioxide molecules, are required to produce one net molecule of glucose.

## Overall significance

Together, the light-dependent and light-independent reactions convert light energy, water, and carbon
dioxide into glucose and oxygen. RuBisCO is considered the most abundant enzyme on Earth because of the
sheer scale of carbon fixation happening in plant life globally. Photosynthesis is the foundation of
nearly all food chains and is responsible for maintaining the oxygen and carbon dioxide balance of
Earth's atmosphere.
