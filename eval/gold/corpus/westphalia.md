# The Peace of Westphalia and the Birth of Modern Sovereignty

The Peace of Westphalia refers to a series of peace treaties signed in 1648 that ended the Thirty
Years' War (1618-1648) in the Holy Roman Empire and the related Eighty Years' War (1568-1648) between
Spain and the Dutch Republic. It consisted of two main treaties: the Treaty of Osnabrück, signed
between the Holy Roman Emperor and Sweden along with their respective allies, and the Treaty of
Münster, signed between the Holy Roman Emperor and France, and separately between Spain and the Dutch
Republic. Both treaties were negotiated simultaneously in the neighboring Westphalian cities of
Osnabrück and Münster, in present-day Germany, which is why the settlement as a whole is known as the
Peace of Westphalia.

## The Thirty Years' War

The Thirty Years' War began in 1618 as a conflict rooted in religious tension between Catholic and
Protestant states within the Holy Roman Empire, sparked by the Defenestration of Prague, but it grew
into a broader European power struggle involving most of the continent's major powers, including
France, Sweden, Spain, and the Habsburg dynasty. It became one of the longest and most destructive
conflicts in European history, causing enormous population loss, particularly within the German
lands, through both direct violence and the famine and disease that followed.

## Key outcomes

The treaties recognized the sovereignty of the Dutch Republic, formally ending Spanish attempts to
reassert control over the Netherlands, and recognized the independence of the Swiss Confederacy from
the Holy Roman Empire. Within the Empire, the settlement granted the constituent territorial rulers
significant autonomy, including the right to determine the official religion of their own territory
(a principle sometimes summarized by the earlier phrase "cuius regio, eius religio"), which curtailed
the practical authority of the Holy Roman Emperor over internal religious and political matters. France,
represented at the negotiations by Cardinal Mazarin, emerged strengthened, gaining territory including
parts of Alsace.

## Westphalian sovereignty

The Peace of Westphalia is widely cited by historians and international relations scholars as marking
the beginning of the modern system of sovereign, territorial nation-states, a concept now commonly
referred to as "Westphalian sovereignty." Under this principle, each state has exclusive authority over
its own territory and domestic affairs, and other states are expected not to interfere in those
internal affairs. While historians debate how sharply the 1648 settlement actually broke with prior
practice, the term "Westphalian system" remains the standard shorthand in international relations
theory for a world order built around independent, formally equal sovereign states rather than
overlapping feudal or religious authorities.

## Legacy

The Peace of Westphalia did not end conflict in Europe, and war between France and Spain, for
instance, continued for another decade after 1648. However, the settlement's framework for resolving
a multi-state conflict through a comprehensive, multilateral peace congress, and its emphasis on state
sovereignty and the balance of power among independent states, influenced the diplomatic practice of
the centuries that followed and is still invoked today as a foundational reference point in discussions
of state sovereignty and international order.
