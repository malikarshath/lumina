# The Dewey Decimal Classification System

The Dewey Decimal Classification (DDC), often called the Dewey Decimal System, is a library
classification system that organizes books and other materials by subject using numbers. It was
devised by the American librarian Melvil Dewey and first published in 1876 in a pamphlet titled "A
Classification and Subject Index for Cataloguing and Arranging the Books and Pamphlets of a Library."
Dewey developed the system while working as a student assistant in the library of Amherst College.

## Structure of the system

The DDC divides all knowledge into ten main classes, numbered 000 through 900, with each class further
divided into ten divisions, and each division into ten sections. The ten main classes are: 000 for
computer science, information, and general works; 100 for philosophy and psychology; 200 for religion;
300 for the social sciences; 400 for language; 500 for science; 600 for technology and applied
sciences; 700 for the arts and recreation; 800 for literature; and 900 for history and geography.

Each subject is assigned a number that can be extended with a decimal point to add further precision,
allowing the classification to be arbitrarily specific. For example, within the 500s (science), 570
covers biology, and a number like 577.8 can be used for a narrower topic such as ecology of a specific
kind of organism. This decimal notation is what gives the system its name, and it allows new subjects
to be inserted into the hierarchy without renumbering existing ones.

## History and stewardship

Dewey based his system on the idea of "relative location," meaning a book's position on the shelf is
determined by its subject rather than by a fixed, permanent shelf number, so the whole collection can
be reshuffled as it grows. This was a significant innovation for its time, when many libraries used
fixed shelf locations. The system spread rapidly among public and school libraries in the United
States and, later, internationally.

In 1988, OCLC (the Online Computer Library Center, a nonprofit library cooperative) acquired the Dewey
Decimal Classification system and has maintained, updated, and published new editions of it ever
since, including an online, continuously updated version called WebDewey. The system remains
particularly popular in public and school libraries, while many academic and research libraries in the
United States instead use the Library of Congress Classification system, which uses a combination of
letters and numbers rather than a purely decimal notation.

## Criticism and adaptation

The DDC has been criticized over the years for reflecting the cultural biases and worldview of its
19th-century American origin, particularly in how it originally organized topics like religion (which
long over-represented Christianity relative to other world religions) and certain social and historical
subjects. OCLC has revised many of these sections in later editions to broaden and rebalance the
classification, and continues to issue updates through WebDewey to keep the system current with new
fields of knowledge, such as computing and emerging technologies.
